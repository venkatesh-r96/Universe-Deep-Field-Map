What's really on this map

Nobody has ever cataloged "every object in the universe" — it isn't possible. The Milky Way alone holds 100–400 billion stars; the observable universe holds an estimated ~2 trillion galaxies. This map is honest about the difference between two kinds of things on it:

1. Real, clickable, data-backed objects —

Every named object you can click — planets, moons, asteroids, comets, stars, exoplanets, pulsars, black holes, star clusters, nebulae, galaxies and superclusters — carries real published figures for mass, distance, temperature and gravity where they're known, researched before this map was built. Some numbers (especially stellar velocities and supercluster masses) are genuinely disputed in astronomy itself, and the map says so where relevant.

2. Illustrative background scatter —

The faint, unlabeled points filling the space between named objects — background stars and a background galaxy field — are procedurally generated to make the map feel as dense as the real sky, using realistic statistical patterns (spiral-arm clustering for stars, filament clustering for galaxies). They are not individually real, cannot be clicked, and don't represent specific catalogued objects.

Zoom out fully to see the edge of the observable universe; zoom into any globular or open cluster to watch it resolve into individual stars.
